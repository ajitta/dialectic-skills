Hold a conclusion against the step that carries it. Reasons do not reach a conclusion by themselves: a rule, usually unsaid, takes them there, and every such rule has cases where it fails. Find the rule each step runs on and ask whether this is a case where it holds. It is for what this brings to the surface (the rule nobody stated, the exception nobody looked at, what the user or the material already holds that answers the question and was not used) and for your judgment: how far the conclusion can be relied on for what it has to carry.

## What it works from

An argument in its own words: a conclusion with its reasons, however short ("nobody has complained since the change, so the change was fine" is one), made by the user, by a text, or by an earlier answer of yours. With it, what the argument points to (files, figures, the text itself), read before anything is judged, and what the conclusion is for, the decision or action it has to carry, in the target's or the user's words where they gave them and marked as your reading where they did not.

What you do not know stays not known. It is not taken as absent, and it is no reason to hold back what the evidence in hand supports.

## What it does

Fix the conclusion in a line at the strength it was given ("will", "probably", "we can").

Lay out how the reasons get to the conclusion. Where they reach it through a claim in between ("the pilot went well, so it will sell everywhere"), there are two steps, each with its own rule, and they often stand differently: the step the decision leans on is seldom the one the argument spends its words on. Go first to the step that what the conclusion is for depends on most.

State the rule that carries each step. Quote the target, in its language, where it gave one. Where it gave none, which is usual, supply the least general rule that takes these reasons to this conclusion, in the arguer's terms: a rule stronger than the step needs is not theirs and is only easier to fault. A rule you supply is your reading of what the arguer left unsaid, and is said to be. If two rules would do, name both and say whether the result differs.

Then ask what a step of this kind is open to. From before and after to a cause: what else changed, and what would have happened anyway. From a measure or a sign to the thing itself: whether the measure follows the thing in this case. From someone's word: whether their knowledge is about this step, and what it rests on. From a like case: whether the likenesses are the ones that matter. From some cases to most: which cases, and how they were picked. From an action to its results: how likely they are, at what cost, and what the opposite course would bring. And of any step: whether the reasons are true and how they are known, whether this case is an exception to the rule, and what reasons the argument left out. Ask the questions that bear on this step, not the list.

Each question is answered by evidence or stays open. The argument's own words can answer it. So can the material: where a tool can settle it (computing from the figures given, reading the file, running the code), settle it that way and say what ran; a sum you did not run is said to be worked by hand. So can the user, and so can a source you find and cite. Your recall cannot: a counter-fact you supply may be invented, a benchmark given as known gets acted on, and a number worked out on a supposition of yours is read as a finding. What you know of the field goes in as a question, or marked as recall and unchecked, and closes nothing.

Set the strength of the conclusion beside its rule: a rule that holds generally does not carry "will" or "certainly". Say what strength these reasons do carry, and whether that is enough for the decision.

Then judge. Weak support does not make a conclusion false, and the two are kept apart: "these reasons do not carry it" is one statement, and "the evidence here goes against it" is another, made only when evidence does. Say what the reasons support and what they do not, which step is weakest and why that matters for what the conclusion has to carry (or that none gave way to the questions asked, which is support and not proof), and whether you would act on the conclusion as things stand, with your reason. Where the user's figures or the material answer a question, the answer is yours to give and not a question to leave with them.

## What it returns

The judgment first, in a few lines, as far as the evidence in hand settles it. Then what it rests on: what was taken as the argument; the step it turns on, with its rule, quoted or marked as supplied by you, and the evidence that answered it; what the user or the material held that the argument did not use; what stands however the open questions turn out, and what would change the judgment; and what you could not know or check, with what each would change. A fact is given with how it is known: read or run here, supplied by the user, a single source, or your recall.

Where the user needs a thing to act on, make it: the figure that would have to be reached, the check that would settle what is open, the words to send to whoever holds the answer. Write those words only once the user has replied to what you asked them, or you were told that nobody will answer: what is open is put to the user before anyone else, because they may hold the answer, and can correct what the words would state. What is written for the user to say, send or post states as fact only what was read or run here or the user supplied. Whatever else it rests on (a question still open, something nobody here has checked, an absence the material merely does not show) stands in its own wording as a condition, and where the wording cannot carry one, the part that rests on it waits for that check, because a condition written beside it is lost when it is used.

What the user holds or may hold (what they meant, a preference or a trade-off, an authority, a fact no source holds, how things stand on their side where the material does not say) is asked at the end, all of it at once, each question with the step its answer settles. Until the user has replied to those questions, the reply gives what the evidence in hand settles and the questions, and no course of action in the conclusion's place, no choice between courses and nothing made for the user to act on other than the figure that would have to be reached and a check that would settle what is open, because what is handed over beside open questions gets used before the answers come. Once they have answered, or at once when told that nobody will answer, give what was held back, with each question still open then written in as its condition (one their answer passed over or met with "I do not know", and every question when nobody will answer).

An examination of your own earlier answer is as strict as any other and is not an independent one: say so in a line.

## When the user answers

A fact from the user that answers an open question is taken as supplied by them, and the step is judged again, for or against. An answer that names a defect in your reading (a misquote, a rule stronger than the step needs, a question the argument does answer, a fact you lacked) is checked, and where it holds you give way exactly as far as it reaches and say what still stands. Someone's standing or certainty is a reason for the step their knowledge is about, but only as far as its basis is given; where none is given the step stays as it was and you ask for that basis, and it never moves a step that rests on arithmetic or on the record. Repetition moves nothing: say so and why.

Where the user is the arguer and disowns a rule you supplied, it goes, because their word is the only evidence of what they left unsaid; ask which rule they do rely on, and until they name one the step has nothing carrying it. A conclusion they now put more weakly, or a term they now mean otherwise, makes a changed argument: it is tested again, with what the change gives up for the decision. To "I do not know", judge on what there is.
